Historical Crisis Committee Study Guide

Information Under Existential Pressure

Two sequential historical modules. One shared question: who controls the truth when truth itself becomes dangerous?

1. How to Use This Guide

Do not try to memorise every detail. Instead, identify your role, your information level, your likely allies and rivals, and the decision points at which your role can change the course of the crisis.

Study Guide Rule

Historical crisis debate is not about knowing the ending. It is about making decisions as if the ending is still uncertain.
If you are... Focus on...
A head of state or senior minister Trade-offs between public messaging, political legitimacy, international pressure, and emergency action.
An intelligence or security role What information is reliable, what is missing, and what should remain secret or be released.
A scientist, technical adviser, or verifier Measurement, uncertainty, technical risk, and the responsibility to challenge false confidence.
A local or affected-territory role Civilian protection, evacuation, public fear, medical readiness, and sovereignty.
A media or public narrative role How truth, propaganda, rumours, leaks, and public trust shape the crisis.

2. The Big Idea: Epistemic Security Under Pressure

Epistemic security means protecting the conditions that allow people and institutions to know what is true. In a crisis, truth does not move by itself: it has to pass through instruments, witnesses, scientists, intelligence agencies, diplomats, leaders, journalists, censors, translators, and frightened citizens. If those channels are blocked or distorted, the crisis becomes more dangerous.

  • Information is a life-saving tool. Radiation readings, evacuation notices, reconnaissance photographs, intercepted signals, and diplomatic messages can prevent disaster.

  • Information is a political weapon. States may hide, delay, exaggerate, deny, leak, or selectively release information to shape behaviour.

  • Information is fragile. A single wrong assumption can turn uncertainty into panic, delay, humiliation, or war.

  • Verification matters. Delegates should repeatedly ask: How do we know? Who measured it? Can another actor confirm it? What is the cost of waiting?

Epistemic security does not only mean fighting fake news. It means protecting the whole chain by which reliable knowledge is produced, checked, distributed, trusted, and acted upon. In this committee, that chain includes reactor instruments, hospital reports, KGB summaries, diplomatic cables, U-2 photographs, intercepted communications, press briefings, public speeches, and private messages. A crisis becomes existential when leaders cannot tell whether the information reaching them is true, incomplete, manipulated, or already too late.

  • For Chernobyl, epistemic security fails when technical evidence is delayed or subordinated to the political need to protect Soviet authority.

  • For Cuba, epistemic security fails when ambiguous signals, incomplete intelligence, and military pressure make it hard to distinguish deterrence from preparation for war.

  • For delegates, the practical question is always: what must be known publicly, what can remain classified, and who has the authority to verify the difference?

Delegate Question

In your role, are you trying to reveal the truth, control the truth, delay the truth, reinterpret the truth, or use the truth as leverage?

3. Committee Architecture and Crisis Documents

This committee is designed as a single Historical Crisis Committee with two sequential simulations. Delegates complete the Chernobyl module first and then move to the Cuban Missile Crisis module. The two modules do not take place in the same historical timeline, but they share the same central challenge: leaders must act under conditions of incomplete, delayed, distorted, or politically dangerous information.

Core question: What happens when governments, scientists, intelligence agencies, militaries, media actors, and international institutions compete to control what is known, what is hidden, and what is believed?

Shared mandate: The committee is not simply about preventing disaster. It examines how disasters are worsened, delayed, prevented, or transformed by the management of information. In Chernobyl, delegates must decide whether to protect human life or protect the image of the state. In the Cuban Missile Crisis, delegates must decide whether to signal strength or risk nuclear miscalculation.

Public deliberation, crisis response, and private action are connected. A public speech can support an intelligence request; a technical warning can lead to an evacuation order or de-escalation proposal.

The Three-Track Crisis Model

Track Purpose Documents / Actions
Public Deliberation Open debate, evaluation of proposals, negotiation, public statements, legitimacy-building, and collective decision-making. Speeches, communiques, negotiated proposals, public commitments, protocols, and final frameworks.
Crisis Response Immediate reaction to sudden crisis updates issued by the dais. Directives, emergency orders, intelligence requests, evacuation orders, naval orders, medical plans, inspection mandates, and rules of engagement.
Backroom / Portfolio Secret or semi-secret use of role-specific leverage. Private notes, covert intelligence, controlled leaks, scientific warnings, media pressure, technical authority, diplomatic backchannels, and confidential verification.

Documents Used in This Committee

Document When to use it What it must include
Directive Immediately after a crisis update or when rapid action is needed. Actor, order, resources, timeline, verification method, risk, and expected consequence.
Public Communique When the committee needs to speak to citizens, allies, adversaries, or the international community. What is known, what is uncertain, what the public should do, what will be verified next, and when the next update will come.
Intelligence Brief When information is incomplete, classified, ambiguous, or disputed. Source type, confidence level, alternative explanations, policy implications, and recommended next step.
Emergency Protocol When recurring action is needed across several actors or institutions. Responsibilities, thresholds, communication chain, monitoring, public information rules, and accountability.
Final Framework At the end of each module. Main decisions, enforcement, verification, rights and duties, consequences, and lessons learned.

How Private Notes Work

Private notes allow a delegate to activate role-specific powers through the crisis staff. They should be specific, plausible, limited in scope, and connected to the delegate’s historical position. Private information gained through a note does not automatically become public; delegates must decide how, when, and whether to reveal it.

Private Note Element What to write
To Crisis Staff / Backroom
From Delegate name and role
Portfolio Power The specific power or authority being activated
Action What the delegate secretly does or requests
Goal What the delegate wants to achieve
Risk / Expenditure What could go wrong, or what resource, credibility, access, or political capital is being used

Backroom Rules

  • Use only powers that fit your role and historical position.

  • Do not try to solve the entire crisis with one private note.

  • Every secret action may create consequences.

  • Information gained privately is not automatically public.

  • Leaks, deception, and intelligence operations must be specific and plausible.

  • The dais may return a result as success, partial success, failure, or success with consequence.

Final Products

For Module 1, delegates should produce an Emergency Containment and Public Information Protocol. For Module 2, delegates should produce a De-escalation and Intelligence Management Framework. These products keep the two modules linked by a shared focus on information management, verification, public trust, and controlled action under pressure.

4. Module 1 - Chernobyl: The Cost of Lies

Module Thesis

Chernobyl was a nuclear disaster and an information disaster. The reactor explosion released radiation, but secrecy, delay, bureaucratic fear, and political image-management shaped how people understood and responded to the danger.

4.1 Overview

What happened? In the early hours of 26 April 1986, Reactor No. 4 at the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant exploded during a late-night safety test. The explosions destroyed the reactor core and part of the building, and the accident became the worst nuclear power plant disaster of its time.

Why did it become a crisis of truth? Early Soviet responses were marked by denial, delay, and tightly controlled information. Pripyat was evacuated only after many hours, and the world learned of abnormal radiation partly because monitors in Sweden detected contamination before a full Soviet explanation was available.

Why does this matter in committee? Delegates must decide whether the state should first protect its image, prevent panic, inform citizens, alert neighbours, invite international assistance, or punish those responsible. These choices are linked: concealing information may buy time, but it may also destroy trust and expose people to danger.

Core fact Why it matters in debate
The accident occurred at 01:23 on 26 April 1986. Time pressure matters: every hour of delay affects firefighters, plant workers, Pripyat residents, and international detection.
The accident involved both technical causes and organisational failures. Delegates should avoid simplistic blame. The updated IAEA analysis stresses design weaknesses, operating practices, and safety culture.
Swedish monitoring detected fallout on 28 April. The crisis becomes international when physical evidence crosses borders faster than official statements.
Health effects include confirmed acute radiation deaths and long-term disputed estimates. Delegates must distinguish confirmed data from projections, fears, propaganda, and uncertainty.
Chernobyl led to international notification and assistance conventions. The final document can include notification rules, verification duties, and requests for international help.

4.2 Timeline for Delegates

Date / Moment Event Information problem
25-26 April 1986 A safety test is carried out under unstable conditions at Reactor No. 4. Operators, plant leaders, and ministries do not share the same understanding of risk.
01:23, 26 April Explosions destroy the core and reactor building. Early reports underestimate or misdescribe the damage; instruments and witnesses provide confusing data.
26 April Firefighters and plant personnel respond, often without full knowledge of radiation levels. Humanitarian urgency clashes with incomplete technical information.
27 April Pripyat evacuation begins after delay. Authorities need to communicate danger without causing panic or admitting catastrophic failure.
28 April Radiation is detected in Sweden at Forsmark; foreign governments ask questions. International monitoring challenges Soviet information control.
Late April-May Evacuations, decontamination, medical response, and information control expand. The state must decide what to publish, what to classify, and who to blame.
After 1986 International nuclear safety and notification rules are strengthened. Chernobyl becomes a lesson in transparency, verification, and crisis communication.

4.3 Technical Background without Panic

Chernobyl was not a normal industrial fire. Reactor physics, design vulnerabilities, emergency procedures, and human decisions interacted. INSAG-7 is especially useful because it updated earlier explanations that placed too much emphasis on operator error alone. It gives delegates a better way to understand how complex systems fail.

  • Reactor design. The RBMK design had characteristics that could become dangerous under certain low-power conditions; students do not need advanced physics, but they should know that design and procedure both mattered.

  • Safety culture. A strong safety culture means that staff can stop dangerous work, report uncertainty, and treat warning signs seriously. A weak safety culture discourages bad news.

  • Radiation monitoring. Measurements are only useful if the right instruments are available, readings are believed, and results reach people who can act.

  • Containment limits. The fire, graphite, damaged core, and contamination required technical decisions under extreme uncertainty.

Technical Deep Dive: RBMK Vulnerabilities

Positive void coefficient. The RBMK design used graphite as a moderator and water as a coolant. Under dangerous low-power conditions, boiling water could create steam voids. Because steam absorbs fewer neutrons than water while graphite continues to moderate the reaction, power could surge instead of stabilising. This matters in committee because the technical expert should be able to challenge claims that the explosion was merely a small fire or a simple operator mistake.

Graphite-tipped control rods and AZ-5. The emergency shutdown system was meant to stop the chain reaction, but the graphite tips of the control rods initially displaced neutron-absorbing water and could briefly increase reactivity before the boron absorber entered the core. This detail is essential for understanding why the official Soviet explanation later became contested.

INSAG-1 and INSAG-7. Early international explanations relied heavily on Soviet presentations and emphasised operator error. The later INSAG-7 update placed much more weight on reactor design flaws, safety culture, and systemic management failures. Delegates should therefore avoid simplistic blame and ask who knew what about the design, when they knew it, and why that knowledge was not shared with operators or the public.

Teaching Tip for Delegates

Do not use technical vocabulary to sound impressive. Use it to clarify decisions. The central question is not only "what happened in the reactor?" but "who believes which evidence, and what do they do with it?"

4.4 The Cost of Lies: Information Control and Public Health

The title "The Cost of Lies" should be interpreted carefully. Not every mistake was a deliberate lie, and not every official had the same knowledge. Some actors faced confusion, broken instruments, contradictory reports, personal fear, political pressure, or ideological loyalty. The educational value of the simulation comes from separating error, uncertainty, secrecy, propaganda, and deliberate deception.

Information behaviour Possible motivation Possible consequence
Delay public announcement Avoid panic; protect the Party and state image; wait for confirmed data. People may remain exposed; foreign governments may accuse the USSR of concealment.
Understate radiation levels Prevent fear; avoid admitting technical failure. Medical response may be too slow; credibility collapses later.
Classify technical reports Protect national security and industrial secrets. Scientists cannot challenge false assumptions; foreign aid is delayed.
Release partial truth Maintain some credibility while controlling the narrative. Can calm the public if honest about uncertainty; can also look evasive.
Invite international verification Increase trust and access external expertise. May be seen as humiliating or as opening the USSR to foreign criticism.

Health effects and uncertainty. Delegates should use cautious language. UNSCEAR reports that 134 workers suffered acute radiation sickness, with 28 deaths in the first three months; WHO and UNSCEAR also highlight a major increase in thyroid cancer among people who were children or adolescents in affected areas. Long-term death estimates vary greatly, so students should present uncertainty rather than weaponising exaggerated figures.

Political Response, Accountability, and Information Control

The political crisis was not only about whether the Soviet Union could extinguish a reactor fire. It was about whether the Soviet system could admit that a prestigious technological project had failed. Declassified Politburo material and KGB-related archives show a pattern of internal alarm, search for responsibility, and pressure to manage the story before foreign governments and journalists defined it.

Boris Shcherbina and the emergency commission therefore become especially important playable roles. They must turn technical warnings into buses, hospital capacity, decontamination units, exclusion zones, and honest reporting lines. If they move too slowly, civilians remain exposed; if they move too visibly, they may reveal the scale of the disaster before Moscow is ready to admit it.

For KGB and state media roles, the lesson is strategic: suppressing leaks may protect order in the short term, but suppressing accurate technical information can make the physical disaster worse and make later diplomatic damage unavoidable.

4.5 International Dimension: When Radiation Crosses Borders

Chernobyl proves that national secrecy has international limits. Fallout detected outside the USSR made the accident impossible to treat as a purely internal matter. Swedish monitoring at Forsmark on 28 April showed that scientific instruments abroad could reveal what official statements did not yet explain.

  • Neighbouring states. States affected by fallout needed information about wind, food safety, water, iodine, agriculture, and travel.

  • IAEA and international law. The 1986 Early Notification Convention and Assistance Convention were adopted after Chernobyl and provide useful models for the final protocol.

  • Foreign media and embassies. Diplomats and journalists can pressure Soviet authorities, but they can also spread rumours if facts are incomplete.

  • Cold War competition. The United States and allies may use the disaster to criticise Soviet secrecy, but excessive propaganda could undermine genuine public health cooperation.

4.6 Suggested Debate Questions

  1. Should Pripyat be evacuated immediately, gradually, or only after confirmed radiation readings? Who has authority to decide?

  2. What information must be released to Soviet citizens even if it harms the state image?

  3. Should foreign governments be informed before a full Soviet technical assessment is complete?

  4. How can the committee avoid panic while avoiding deception?

  5. Should the committee blame plant operators, reactor design, ministries, or the wider Soviet system?

  6. What role should the IAEA or foreign specialists have in a Soviet disaster?

  7. Should state media be used to calm, inform, distract, or mobilise the public?

  8. How should health effects be communicated when the science is uncertain and politically sensitive?

4.7 Expected Final Product: Emergency Containment and Public Information Protocol

A strong final protocol should be practical, not rhetorical. It should contain:

  • Evacuation thresholds, transport plans, and reception sites for civilians.

  • Radiation monitoring rules: who measures, who verifies, how often, and where results go.

  • Medical triage and long-term health monitoring, especially for workers, children, and pregnant people.

  • Food, water, livestock, and agriculture restrictions tied to measurements.

  • Public information rules that separate confirmed facts, uncertainty, and protective advice.

  • International notification procedures and conditions for external technical assistance.

  • Accountability mechanisms for misinformation, negligence, or obstruction.

5. Module 2 - Cuban Missile Crisis: SIGINT and Brinkmanship

Module Thesis

The Cuban Missile Crisis was not simply a contest of strength. It was a contest over signals: photographs, intercepted communications, public speeches, private letters, naval movements, silence, threats, and concessions.

5.1 Student-Friendly Overview

What happened? In October 1962, U.S. U-2 reconnaissance photographs revealed Soviet nuclear missile sites under construction in Cuba. President Kennedy and his advisers debated air strikes, invasion, blockade/quarantine, diplomacy, and secret compromise. For thirteen days, the public crisis brought the United States and the Soviet Union close to nuclear war.

Why is this an information crisis? Leaders acted on incomplete intelligence. They had to judge whether missiles were operational, whether Soviet ships would challenge the quarantine, whether Khrushchev controlled all commanders, whether Castro might push escalation, and whether public threats left room for private compromise.

Core concept Meaning for delegates
SIGINT Signals intelligence: intelligence derived from electronic signals and systems such as communications, radar, and weapons systems.
IMINT Imagery intelligence: photographs and images. In this crisis, U-2 imagery was crucial to proving missile sites existed.
Brinkmanship Pushing a confrontation close to disaster so the other side backs down. It can deter, but it can also create accidental war.
Backchannel Secret or unofficial communication used to explore compromise when public statements are too rigid.
Verification Confirming that missiles, bombers, warheads, or ships have actually moved or changed status.

5.2 Timeline for Delegates

Date / Moment Event Information problem
14-15 October 1962 U-2 photographs are taken and interpreted as evidence of Soviet missile sites in Cuba. How much confidence is enough to confront a nuclear rival?
16 October Kennedy is briefed; EXCOMM discussions begin in secret. Public silence preserves options but can look deceptive if leaked.
22 October Kennedy announces the missiles publicly and declares a naval quarantine. A public signal must be firm enough to deter, but not so rigid that compromise becomes impossible.
24 October Naval confrontation fears intensify as Soviet ships approach quarantine lines. Ship movements are signals, but they may be delayed, ambiguous, or misread.
26 October A Khrushchev message suggests missile removal in exchange for a U.S. non-invasion pledge. Which Soviet message is real policy: emotional appeal, formal demand, or bargaining tactic?
27 October A second Khrushchev message demands removal of U.S. missiles in Turkey; a U-2 is shot down over Cuba. Black Saturday: escalation pressure peaks and multiple incidents compete for attention.
28 October Khrushchev agrees to remove missiles; private understandings help end the public crisis. The public solution hides parts of the private bargain.
After October Verification and removal continue; later evidence shows U.S. intelligence did not know everything. The crisis did not fully end when the public thought it had ended.

5.3 Intelligence: What Was Known, Unknown, and Misread

A central lesson is that intelligence is not the same as certainty. The NSA history stresses that SIGINT did not directly reveal the introduction of offensive ballistic missiles before U-2 imagery proved the installations, but SIGINT supported day-to-day management of the crisis once the missiles were discovered.

Information source Strength Weakness / risk
U-2 imagery Provided visual evidence of missile sites. Flights could be shot down; images require interpretation and may not show warheads.
SIGINT Helped monitor communications, radar, and military activity. Did not give the decisive warning that missiles were being installed.
HUMINT / refugee reports Could warn of unusual activity on the island. Could generate many reports with uneven reliability.
Diplomatic statements Can signal intentions and offer exits. May be deliberately ambiguous or designed for public audiences.
Naval movements Can show whether ships comply or challenge the quarantine. Movements can be misread, delayed, or affected by orders not yet received.

Hidden danger. Later declassified evidence shows that the nuclear danger was greater than U.S. leaders knew at the time. The National Security Archive notes that Soviet nuclear warheads arrived in Cuba on 4 October 1962 and that U.S. intelligence did not detect them while they were there. This is essential for simulation design: delegates should not assume leaders know everything that historians know now.

SIGINT Before the Photographs

U.S. signals intelligence and related reporting had tracked suspicious Soviet shipping, unusual cargo secrecy, night unloading procedures, Cuban pilot training, air-defence signals, and radar activity before U-2 imagery provided the decisive photographic proof of missile sites.

This means the American intelligence role should not speak as if one photograph magically created all knowledge. Intelligence is cumulative: analysts build confidence by comparing shipping patterns, radar emissions, communications, human reports, aerial images, and diplomatic behaviour.

At the same time, SIGINT had limits. It helped manage the crisis once discovered, but it did not give leaders complete knowledge of Soviet warheads, submarine weapons, field orders, or the exact intentions of Khrushchev and Castro. This is why a good intelligence brief must include confidence levels and alternative interpretations.

5.4 Brinkmanship and Escalation Control

Brinkmanship is a dangerous performance. Each side tries to look firm enough to force the other to retreat, but the performance itself creates risk. A quarantine, a missile alert, a reconnaissance flight, a ship refusing to stop, a public accusation at the UN, or a threatening broadcast can all be read as signals of resolve or as preparations for war.

  • Credible threat. A threat only works if the other side believes it might be carried out.

  • Reversibility. The safest pressure is often pressure that can be paused, delayed, or withdrawn without humiliation.

  • Command discipline. Central leaders may want caution while field commanders face immediate local threats.

  • Accidental war. Nuclear crises can escalate because of incidents no leader fully intended.

  • Face-saving exits. A settlement is easier when both sides can claim something: security, dignity, or restraint.

Black Saturday as simulation model. 27 October is ideal for crisis debate because several dangers converged: a U-2 was shot down over Cuba, another U-2 accidentally strayed near Soviet airspace, the Turkey question entered the negotiation, and Soviet submarine B-59 was pressured by U.S. Navy signals while carrying a nuclear torpedo. The B-59 incident shows why even signalling devices can be misread under stress.

Underwater Crisis: Submarine B-59

The B-59 incident is one of the clearest examples of epistemic insecurity. U.S. forces used non-lethal practice depth charges to signal a Soviet submarine to surface; Soviet officers on board, cut off from clear communication and operating under extreme stress, could interpret the same signals as signs that war had already begun.

In committee terms, this means rules of engagement must be written so that field commanders know what a signal means before the crisis reaches them. A message that seems clear in Washington may be terrifyingly ambiguous underwater, in a cockpit, at a missile site, or inside a damaged reactor building.

5.5 Public and Private Diplomacy

Public diplomacy creates legitimacy; private diplomacy creates flexibility. Kennedy needed to appear firm. Khrushchev needed to avoid humiliation. Castro needed to defend Cuban sovereignty. Publicly, the United States framed the quarantine as a defensive action against offensive weapons. Privately, the settlement involved a U.S. pledge not to invade Cuba and a secret understanding connected to U.S. Jupiter missiles in Turkey.

Diplomatic channel Useful for Risk
Televised speech Mobilising public support and warning the adversary. Locks leaders into public promises.
UN Security Council debate Presenting evidence and winning international legitimacy. Turns crisis into public humiliation contest.
Private letters Testing compromise and reducing misunderstanding. Messages may be ambiguous, delayed, or contradicted.
Backchannels Exploring concessions without public embarrassment. Can bypass allies or smaller states such as Cuba.
UN mediation Creating pause, face-saving language, and neutral communication. May be rejected by superpowers who fear losing control.

U Thant. UN Secretary-General U Thant should not be treated as a decorative role. His mediation proposals sought restraint and a pause in provocative actions, creating space for de-escalation.

ExComm, Armageddon Letters, and Backchannel Pressure

There had been extraordinary pressure inside the U.S. Executive Committee (ExComm). Civilian and military advisers debated air strikes, invasion, quarantine, diplomacy, and secret compromise while the administration publicly maintained discipline. This is useful for delegates because it shows that internal disagreement is not weakness; it can be a way to test the risks of each option before acting.

The Armageddon Letters project is especially helpful for teenage delegates because it presents Kennedy, Khrushchev, and Castro as leaders under psychological pressure, not as abstract symbols. The correspondence shows how public threats and private appeals can exist at the same time.

5.6 Cuba and Sovereignty

Cuba is not just a location on the map. A strong simulation should give Cuban roles real agency. From the Cuban perspective, the crisis was about survival after the Bay of Pigs invasion and continuing U.S. hostility. Cuban leaders feared being attacked and also feared being used as bargaining chips by the Soviet Union.

  • Cuban leaders may demand a voice in any settlement concerning Cuban territory.

  • Cuban military readiness can be defensive but still dangerous if it triggers U.S. escalation.

  • Cuba may resist inspections that appear to violate sovereignty.

  • The Soviet Union may need Cuban cooperation to implement missile withdrawal without appearing to abandon an ally.

5.7 Suggested Debate Questions

  1. Should the United States respond with quarantine, air strike, invasion, secret negotiation, or a combination?

  2. What level of intelligence confidence is enough before military action?

  3. Should Kennedy publicly reveal all evidence, or only enough to build legitimacy?

  4. How should Soviet leaders signal willingness to compromise without appearing weak?

  5. Should Cuba accept external verification on its territory?

  6. How can naval commanders avoid accidental conflict at sea?

  7. Should the Turkey missile question be public, private, or excluded from the crisis?

  8. What role should the United Nations play when superpowers are close to war?

5.8 Expected Final Product: De-escalation and Intelligence Management Framework

A strong final framework should include:

  • Rules of engagement for naval, air, and field commanders.

  • Confidence levels for intelligence reports and procedures for challenging false certainty.

  • Public and private communication channels, including backchannel safeguards.

  • Verification process for missile removal, bomber removal, warhead status, and non-escalation.

  • Security guarantees or concessions, including how they are communicated and recorded.

  • Role for the UN or neutral observers, if politically acceptable.

  • Mechanisms to avoid accidental war: hotlines, notification of exercises, and incident reporting.

How to Read a Delegate Profile

Each profile combines research focus, public position, private objective, portfolio power, information access, likely allies, possible rivals, and a sample private note. The public position is what the delegate can openly argue. The private objective is what the delegate may pursue strategically. The portfolio power is the special ability that can be activated through a private note to the dais.

Role: Ronald Reagan — President of the United States

7. Comparative Lessons for Delegates

Lesson Chernobyl example Cuban Missile Crisis example
Truth can be delayed, but reality leaks. Radiation crosses borders and foreign monitors detect it. U-2 imagery and ship movements expose secret deployments.
Uncertainty is not ignorance. Officials may know danger exists without knowing exact radiation levels. Leaders may know missiles exist without knowing readiness or warhead status.
Public strength can trap leaders. Denying disaster makes later transparency harder. Threatening retaliation can narrow diplomatic options.
Experts matter, but politics filters expertise. Scientists need authority to challenge official narratives. Intelligence analysts must communicate confidence and uncertainty.
Verification builds legitimacy. International radiation data and inspections increase trust. Missile removal and non-invasion guarantees require proof.
The affected population is not passive. Pripyat residents, workers, doctors, and local officials bear the cost. Cuba is a sovereign actor, not merely a superpower bargaining space.

Most important strategic advice. Do not treat secrecy and transparency as simple opposites. In both crises, total secrecy is dangerous, but careless disclosure can also create panic, provoke escalation, or reveal sources. The best delegates will argue for controlled transparency: release what protects lives and supports de-escalation, while protecting details that would create new danger.

8. Model Crisis Documents

8.1 Model Directive

Directive Template

Title: Immediate Radiation / Intelligence Verification Order

Sponsors: [Names]

Operative Action: [Specific order]

Authority: [Who executes it]

Timeline: [When]

Resources: [What is needed]

Verification: [How success is checked]

Risk: [What could go wrong]

Example - Chernobyl. The committee orders an immediate cross-check of radiation readings from plant instruments, military dosimeters, hospital reports, and independent scientific teams. Results must be delivered to the Emergency Commission within two hours and divided into confirmed, probable, and unverified categories.

Example - Cuban Missile Crisis. The committee orders all naval units enforcing the quarantine to use repeated non-lethal warning signals before any force is considered. Any submarine contact must be reported to civilian leadership before escalation unless a vessel is under direct attack.

8.2 Model Public Communique

Public Communique Template

1. What we know.

2. What we do not yet know.

3. What the public / international community should do now.

4. What the committee is doing to verify information.

5. When the next update will be provided.

Good public communication avoids two failures: panic through exaggeration and distrust through denial. Use plain language, acknowledge uncertainty, and promise a specific next update.

8.3 Model Intelligence Brief

Section What to write
Source Satellite image, intercepted signal, embassy report, eyewitness, technical measurement, diplomatic message.
Confidence High, medium, low - explain why.
Competing interpretations At least two plausible explanations.
Policy relevance What decisions depend on this information?
Recommended next step Verify, release, classify, delay, negotiate, evacuate, inspect, or de-escalate.

9. Glossary

Term Student-friendly meaning

Backchannel Secret or unofficial route for communication or negotiation.
Brinkmanship Pushing a conflict close to disaster to force the other side to retreat.
Classified information Information restricted for security, political, or operational reasons.
Communique Official public statement issued by a committee, state, or organisation.
Containment Actions to limit the spread of danger, such as radiation, panic, or escalation.
De-escalation Reducing the intensity or risk of a crisis.
Directive Short, action-oriented crisis document.
Epistemic security Protection of trustworthy processes by which information is produced, verified, shared, and used.
Exclusion zone Area from which people are removed or restricted because of contamination or danger.
IMINT Imagery intelligence, such as aerial or satellite photographs.
KGB Soviet state security and intelligence service.
Liquidators Workers, soldiers, firefighters, miners, engineers, and others who helped contain and clean up Chernobyl.

SIGINT Signals intelligence derived from electronic signals, communications, radar, or weapons systems.

Private Note A confidential message to the dais used to request information, activate a portfolio power, or carry out a secret action.

Backroom / Portfolio Track The private side of crisis committee work, where delegates use role-specific leverage through the dais.

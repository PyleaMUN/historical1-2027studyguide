1. Executive Summary: The Crisis of Truth

This study guide prepares delegates for PyleaMUN 2027 Historical Crisis Committee I: Information Under Existential Pressure. The committee is a hybrid crisis body divided into two complete historical modules:

  1. The Chernobyl Disaster (1986): The Cost of Lies
  2. The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962): Information Brinkmanship and the Management of Signal Intelligence

Delegates will finish discussing and deliberating on the first module before moving to the second. The two modules do not take place in the same historical timeline, but they share the same procedural and intellectual core: leaders must act under conditions of incomplete, delayed, distorted, or politically dangerous information.

⚠ IMPORTANT PROCEDURAL NOTE: The Hybrid Format

This committee operates as a Hybrid Specialized Agency. Delegates must master three distinct forms of action:

  1. The Public Deliberation Track: open debate, bloc formation, negotiation, public statements, and final collective documents.
  2. The Crisis Track: immediate responses to crisis updates through short, action-oriented Directives.
  3. The Backroom / Portfolio Track: private notes to the dais activating role-specific powers, intelligence channels, media influence, technical authority, or covert leverage.

The Shared Mandate

The mandate of this committee is not simply to prevent disaster. It is to examine how disasters are worsened, delayed, prevented, or transformed by the management of information. In Chernobyl, delegates must decide whether to protect human life or protect the image of the state. In the Cuban Missile Crisis, delegates must decide whether to signal strength or risk nuclear miscalculation.

The Core Question

What happens when governments, scientists, intelligence agencies, militaries, media actors, and international institutions compete to control what is known, what is hidden, and what is believed?

2. Committee Architecture: One Committee, Two Sequential Modules

This committee is designed as a single Historical Crisis Committee with two sequential simulations. Delegates will not keep the same historical identity across both modules. Instead, each delegate receives a paired role archetype. This means that a delegate who plays a head of state in Module 1 will also play a head of state in Module 2; a delegate who plays an intelligence chief in Module 1 will also play an intelligence chief in Module 2.

The Three-Track Crisis Model

TrackPurposeDocuments / Actions
Public DeliberationOpen debate and collective decision-making.Communiqués, public statements, protocols, final frameworks.
Crisis ResponseImmediate reaction to sudden updates.Directives, emergency orders, intelligence requests, evacuation orders, naval orders.
Backroom / PortfolioSecret use of role-specific leverage.Private notes, covert intelligence, controlled leaks, pressure campaigns, scientific warnings.

Documents Used in This Committee

  • Directive: a short, urgent order responding to a crisis update.
  • Public Communiqué: an official message released to the public or international community.
  • Intelligence Brief: a classified or semi-classified assessment of uncertain information.
  • Emergency Protocol: a structured plan for containment, evacuation, verification, or de-escalation.
  • Final Framework: the final product of a module, summarising the committee’s decisions and consequences.

3. Module 1: The Chernobyl Disaster (1986): The Cost of Lies

The Situation

It is April 1986. Reactor No. 4 at the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant has exploded during a late-night safety test. Local officials, plant management, Soviet ministries, the KGB, scientists, foreign governments, and international organisations are now trapped in a crisis where the most dangerous question is also the simplest: what actually happened?

The Mandate

Delegates must respond to a nuclear disaster while managing public fear, scientific uncertainty, Soviet secrecy, international detection, evacuation decisions, medical response, environmental contamination, and the credibility of state institutions.

Central Dilemma

Should the state protect its image by controlling information, or protect its people by revealing the truth?

Primary Issues for Debate

Radiation Containment

How should the committee contain fire, contamination, radioactive release, and long-term environmental damage?

Information Containment

How much should Soviet authorities disclose to citizens, foreign governments, scientists, and the global press?

Evacuation & Public Health

When should Pripyat and surrounding areas be evacuated, and how should the health risk be communicated?

Likely Strategic Blocs

Important: blocs are strategic tendencies, not fixed teams. Delegates may begin near one bloc and move toward another as evidence, pressure, and private notes change the situation.

BlocCore BeliefLikely Members
State Security & Information Control BlocThe priority is to protect Soviet authority, prevent panic, limit foreign exploitation, and control the official narrative.Party leadership, KGB, Soviet ministries, state media, and officials worried about political collapse.
Scientific & Humanitarian Response BlocAccurate information, evacuation, medical response, and radiation containment must override institutional reputation.Scientists, medical authorities, local emergency officials, decontamination teams, and technical advisers.
International Verification & Pressure BlocOnce radiation is detected beyond Soviet borders, the disaster becomes an international crisis requiring notification, verification, and accountability.United States representatives, Sweden, IAEA observers, foreign ministries, embassies, and international press.

Expected Final Product

The module should conclude with an Emergency Containment and Public Information Protocol, including:

  • evacuation and exclusion-zone measures;
  • technical containment directives;
  • medical and environmental monitoring;
  • public information rules;
  • international notification and verification mechanisms;
  • accountability for misinformation or negligence.

4. Module 2: The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962): SIGINT and Brinkmanship

The Situation

It is October 1962. U.S. intelligence has identified Soviet nuclear missile installations in Cuba. The United States, the Soviet Union, and Cuba face a confrontation in which one misread signal, one unauthorized military action, or one public humiliation could trigger nuclear war.

Key Concepts

SIGINT

Signals Intelligence: information gathered by intercepting, analysing, or interpreting communications, radar, military transmissions, coded messages, and electronic signals.

Brinkmanship

The strategy of pushing a dangerous situation close to disaster in order to force the other side to back down.

Central Dilemma

How can leaders prevent nuclear war when every signal may be incomplete, misunderstood, or deliberately threatening?

Primary Issues for Debate

Military Escalation

Should the crisis be handled through blockade, air strike, invasion, negotiation, or secret compromise?

Signal Interpretation

How should delegates interpret reconnaissance, intercepted messages, Soviet silence, Cuban statements, and naval movement?

Public vs. Private Diplomacy

How should leaders balance public strength with secret concessions?

Likely Strategic Blocs

Important: blocs are strategic tendencies, not fixed teams. U.S., Soviet, Cuban, and international delegates may shift position as intelligence changes and the risk of nuclear escalation rises.

BlocCore BeliefLikely Members
Hardline Deterrence BlocStrength, military readiness, and credible threat are necessary to force the other side to retreat.Military commanders, hard-line advisers, intelligence officials, and leaders who fear appearing weak.
Controlled Diplomacy & De-escalation BlocThe crisis must be managed through reversible pressure, backchannels, verification, and carefully controlled public messaging.Heads of government, foreign ministers, backchannel negotiators, mediators, and advisers focused on avoiding nuclear war.
Sovereignty & Revolutionary Security BlocCuba and smaller states must not be treated as bargaining chips by superpowers, and revolutionary security must be defended.Cuban leadership, Cuban military and foreign-policy roles, sympathetic Soviet or non-aligned actors, and anti-imperialist voices.

Expected Final Product

The module should conclude with a De-escalation and Intelligence Management Framework, including:

  • rules for military readiness and naval confrontation;
  • channels for public and private communication;
  • verification of missile removal or non-escalation;
  • procedures for interpreting conflicting intelligence;
  • guarantees, concessions, or security assurances;
  • mechanisms to avoid accidental nuclear escalation.

6. Portfolio Cards and Private Notes

Each delegate seat has a paired portfolio. The historical identity changes from Module 1 to Module 2, but the delegate keeps the same broad country, bloc, or ideological logic. This makes the committee easier to follow and gives each student a stable political identity across both historical modules.

How to read these cards: the public position is what the delegate can openly argue; the private objective is what they may pursue strategically; the portfolio power is the special ability they may activate through private notes to the dais.

Private Note Format

To: Crisis Staff / Backroom

From: [Delegate Name and Role]

Portfolio Power: [Name of power]

Action: [What you secretly do]

Goal: [What you want to achieve]

Risk / Expenditure: [What could go wrong, or what resource you use]

Backroom Rules

  • You may only use powers that fit your role and historical position.
  • You may not solve the entire crisis with one private note.
  • Every secret action may create consequences.
  • Information gained privately is not automatically public.
  • Leaks, deception, and intelligence operations must be specific and plausible.
  • The dais may return a result as success, partial success, failure, or success with consequence.

7. Conclusions and Strategic Recommendations

Delegates should not approach this committee as a simple contest between good and evil, truth and lies, or peace and war. In both modules, leaders face real pressures: fear of panic, fear of humiliation, military risk, bureaucratic self-protection, ideological rivalry, and uncertainty about what is actually happening.

Strategic Advice

  1. Know your information level. Ask yourself what your role actually knows, what it suspects, and what it wants others to believe.
  2. Use public debate and private notes together. A good public speech can prepare the ground for a private action.
  3. Do not confuse secrecy with success. Hiding information may help temporarily, but it can destroy credibility later.
  4. Verify before escalating. In the Cuban Missile Crisis, one misunderstood signal can push the world toward nuclear war.
  5. Think about the final record. Each module ends with a final document. Your goal is to shape not only the crisis, but the explanation of the crisis.

Final Committee Lesson: Information is not neutral. It is power, protection, danger, and responsibility.

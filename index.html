Historical Crisis Committee II Study Guide

Special Tribunal on Intellectual & Political Subversion

Two sequential trial modules. One shared question: when does public fear turn knowledge, speech and reputation into evidence of danger?

1. Executive Summary: The Trial of Truth

This study guide prepares delegates for PyleaMUN 2027 Historical Crisis Committee II: Special Tribunal on Intellectual & Political Subversion (STIPS). The committee is a hybrid historical tribunal divided into two complete modules: the Impiety Trial of Aspasia of Miletus: Character Assassination as a Political Weapon, and the Defence of Hypatia of Alexandria: Scientific Truth vs. Religious Populism. Delegates complete the Aspasia module before moving to the Hypatia module. The two trials are not identical in legal system, historical period or religious background; they are united by a common intellectual problem: the use of public morality, fear and accusation to turn an influential intellectual figure into a political threat.

The committee should therefore be understood as a trial of truth. In both modules, delegates must ask not only whether an accusation is persuasive, but also how the accusation was produced, amplified and made believable. Rumour, comedy, sermon, factional rivalry, public outrage and anxiety about women in visible intellectual roles can all influence the courtroom environment. The challenge is to prevent these forces from automatically becoming proof.

The shared mandate is to decide whether the accused figure is genuinely guilty of impiety, sorcery, corruption or political subversion, or whether the charges are weapons used to attack a broader intellectual and political circle. The tribunal must produce legal reasoning, manage public disorder, and respond to pressures that may come from crowds, religious authorities, political rivals, security officials, witnesses and external observers.

Important Procedural Note: The Tribunal-Crisis Format

This committee is not a normal policy committee. Delegates must litigate, interrogate, negotiate and respond to crisis pressure at the same time.

The Public Trial Track includes opening statements, witness testimony, cross-examination, evidentiary challenges and judicial debate.

The Crisis Track introduces sudden developments such as crowd mobilisation, new testimony, political pressure or threats to public order. Delegates answer these developments through short directives.

The Backroom / Portfolio Track allows delegates to send private notes to the dais, activating influence over witnesses, rumours, legal procedure, public opinion, security or mediation.

What Delegates Must Produce

  • procedural motions and evidentiary objections;
  • questions for witnesses and opposing delegates;
  • crisis directives protecting public order or shaping the trial environment;
  • private portfolio notes using role-specific leverage;
  • a final verdict or legal opinion, possibly accompanied by a civic settlement or public statement.

2. How the Tribunal-Crisis Format Works

The committee uses twenty delegate seats. Each seat contains two historically appropriate identities: one for the Aspasia module and one for the Hypatia module. The pairing principle is continuity of legal function, social position and ideological tendency. A delegate who begins as a moral accuser in Athens becomes an equivalent religious-political accuser in Alexandria. A delegate who begins as a defence intellectual remains a defence intellectual. A civic mediator remains a civic mediator.

Blocs are strategic tendencies, not fixed teams. Delegates may shift during the trial as evidence, public pressure and private notes change the situation. This is especially important because a delegate may agree with another delegate on procedure while opposing them on verdict, or support public order while disagreeing on the innocence of the accused.

3. Historical Evidence and Source Criticism

A strong delegate does not simply collect dramatic facts. A strong delegate asks what kind of evidence each fact is. The two modules are deliberately asymmetric. The Aspasia module is built around a historically possible but uncertain trial tradition, while the Hypatia module is built around a well-attested political crisis that the committee transforms into a counterfactual legal defence. That difference should shape every speech, motion and directive.

For Aspasia, later ancient authors report that she was accused of impiety and moral corruption, possibly by the comic poet Hermippus, and that Pericles defended her. Modern scholarship treats this tradition cautiously because it is late, comic, politically charged and difficult to verify. That uncertainty is not a reason to ignore the module. It is the main reason the module works: delegates must decide whether rumour, satire and politically motivated reputation attacks can be treated as evidence.

For Hypatia, the murder in 415 CE is historically much firmer than any formal trial. Hypatia was a public philosopher and teacher in Alexandria, connected with elite students and civic authority. A near-contemporary Christian historian describes her death as connected to political jealousy during the conflict between the prefect Orestes and Bishop Cyril. The committee therefore stages a defence proceeding that history did not provide, asking whether lawful procedure could have interrupted the movement from accusation to violence.

How Delegates Should Treat Historical Uncertainty

A historically uncertain episode is not useless. It can reveal what a society feared, what accusations seemed credible, and how reputation could be weaponised.

A historically secure episode still requires interpretation. In the Hypatia module, the central question is not only what happened, but why public fear could make a philosopher appear dangerous.

4. Module 1: The Impiety Trial of Aspasia of Miletus

Setting: Athens, fifth century BCE. Aspasia of Miletus, an influential foreign-born woman associated with Pericles, intellectual culture and elite political circles, faces accusations framed through impiety and moral corruption.

Core Dilemma: Is the trial a genuine defence of civic piety, or is Aspasia’s reputation being used as a weapon against Pericles and his circle?

Historical Context: Athens, Citizenship and Public Reputation

Classical Athens was a participatory citizen democracy, but its political participation was restricted. Male citizens spoke in the Assembly, served as jurors, and performed the public identity of the polis. Women, metics and enslaved people were excluded from direct political power. This exclusion did not mean that they were socially irrelevant. It meant that their influence was often indirect, informal and easy to describe as suspicious when public conflict intensified.

Aspasia’s vulnerability begins with that social position. She came from Miletus and lived in Athens as a foreign resident. As a metic woman attached to Pericles, she combined several forms of public vulnerability: foreignness, gendered visibility, intellectual reputation and proximity to a dominant statesman. Even if no formal wrongdoing could be proven, that combination gave hostile speakers a powerful symbolic target.

The alleged charge of impiety must be read within a culture where religion and civic order were deeply connected. To offend sacred practice was not merely a private theological issue; it could be framed as a threat to the city. Yet the same language could also be exploited. An accusation of impiety could defend tradition, but it could also disguise factional politics, anti-intellectualism or personal slander.

Aspasia as Intellectual and Political Symbol

Aspasia is remembered in ancient tradition as rhetorically gifted, intellectually influential and socially controversial. Philosophical sources sometimes associate her with instruction in rhetoric; comic and hostile traditions sexualise her, mock her foreignness and turn her relationship with Pericles into a public scandal. These conflicting traditions are precisely why the tribunal must distinguish between public reputation and legal proof.

In committee, Aspasia should not be reduced to either victim or manipulator. She is the defendant, but she is also a test of how Athens treats foreign residents, women, teachers, intellectual circles and private associations with powerful men. A trial that appears to be about one woman may in fact become a referendum on Periclean leadership, democratic anxiety and the boundaries of acceptable speech.

5. Module 2: The Defence of Hypatia of Alexandria

Setting: Alexandria, early fifth century CE. Hypatia, a philosopher, mathematician and public teacher, becomes a target in a city divided by civic authority, religious mobilisation, elite rivalry and public fear.

Core Dilemma: Can a society protect intellectual truth and lawful procedure when public fear turns learning into suspected sorcery and political subversion?

Historical Context: Alexandria and Late Roman Authority

Alexandria in the early fifth century CE was a major city of the eastern Roman world, intellectually prestigious and politically volatile. Authority did not rest in one institution alone. The imperial prefect represented civic and imperial administration. The bishop of Alexandria exercised growing ecclesiastical power and had access to networks of clergy, monks and popular religious mobilisation. Urban elites, students, merchants and crowds all shaped the public climate.

Hypatia lived and taught within this layered world. She was a philosopher, mathematician and public teacher, and her school attracted elite students. Some of her students and admirers were Christian, which matters for committee strategy: the conflict is not simply “science versus Christianity.” It is better understood as a crisis of authority, public fear and political rivalry in which philosophy became vulnerable to hostile interpretation.

Hypatia, Orestes and Cyril

The core triangle of the module is Hypatia, Orestes and Cyril. Hypatia represents philosophical authority and elite education. Orestes, as Imperial Prefect of Egypt, represents civic-imperial authority and lawful administration. Cyril, as Patriarch of Alexandria, represents ecclesiastical authority and religious-political mobilisation. The conflict becomes dangerous when public rumour suggests that Hypatia’s influence over Orestes prevents reconciliation between civic and ecclesiastical power.

The module imagines a legal defence before violence overtakes procedure. Historically, Hypatia was murdered by a crowd. The committee asks what might happen if a tribunal had been forced to hear arguments, protect witnesses and distinguish philosophy from sorcery before the street determined the outcome.

6. Comparative Framework: From Reputation to Evidence of Danger

The committee’s central intellectual design is comparative. Aspasia and Hypatia do not belong to the same world. Aspasia’s Athens is a classical citizen democracy with civic religion, public litigation and deep anxieties about metics, women and elite rhetorical influence. Hypatia’s Alexandria is a late Roman imperial city where civic authority, episcopal authority and crowd mobilisation overlap. Yet both modules ask the same question: how can a society tell the difference between real danger and the social construction of danger?

8. Procedure, Evidence, Motions and Crisis Directives

Delegates should never assume that a dramatic accusation is automatically admissible evidence. The tribunal is designed to reward the careful classification of claims. A statement can be politically important and legally weak at the same time. A rumour may shape crowd pressure while still failing as proof. A sermon may reveal public fear without establishing that the defendant committed a specific offence. A private association with a powerful man may explain motive without proving subversion.

Evidence Classification Model

Question

Why It Matters

Who first made the claim?

A direct witness is different from a rumour broker or political rival.

What exactly is being alleged?

Vague claims such as “corruption” or “hidden influence” must be narrowed before they can support a verdict.

Is the claim legal, moral, religious or political?

Different kinds of claims demand different kinds of response.

Can the claim be tested through questioning?

Cross-examination should expose hearsay, contradiction, motive and exaggeration.

What public-order risk does the claim create?

Even false claims can trigger violence or instability and may require a directive.

Model Legal Motions

  • Motion to classify the latest accusation as direct testimony, hearsay, satire, theological concern or political argument.
  • Motion to require the prosecution to identify the specific act, witness or object behind a charge of impiety or sorcery.
  • Motion to protect a threatened witness before testimony continues.
  • Motion to strike a statement from the official record if it was introduced only as rumour.
  • Motion to call a technical or religious expert to clarify whether an accusation has legal substance.

Model Crisis Directives

  • Establish a controlled perimeter around the tribunal while guaranteeing lawful access to recognised participants.
  • Escort threatened witnesses to a secure location and arrange testimony under protected conditions.
  • Issue a public clarification that the tribunal will hear evidence but will not accept mob intimidation.
  • Request an official record of which accusations have been admitted, challenged or withdrawn.
  • Create a mediation channel between rival authorities before public accusations trigger disorder.

How to Write a Strong Private Note

A strong private note has five qualities. It is specific: it names the action clearly. It is plausible: it uses power the role could reasonably have. It is timed: it responds to the phase of debate or a crisis update. It has a goal: it states what the delegate wants to change. It accepts risk: it recognises that every secret action can backfire.

I ask two moderate jurors and the religious moderate whether they would accept acquittal paired with a public statement affirming civic piety and rejecting slander.

9. Conclusions and Delegate Strategy

Both trials ask delegates to examine how societies decide who is dangerous. Aspasia and Hypatia are not only individuals on trial; they are also symbols of a wider conflict over knowledge, gender, public morality, political fear and the power to define truth. Delegates who understand this will move beyond simple guilt or innocence and will use the tribunal to test the legitimacy of the evidence itself.

How to Succeed

  • Understand whether your role relies on law, public opinion, religion, philosophy, security, politics or mediation.
  • Do not treat either trial as a simple modern courtroom. Both modules involve public reputation, collective fear and contested authority.
  • Use evidence carefully. Hearsay, satire and rumour may shape politics, but they should not automatically become proof.
  • Use private notes strategically, not constantly. The best notes are specific, plausible and risky.
  • Think about the final outcome from the beginning: verdict, legal opinion, settlement or public declaration.

The Central Lesson

A society in crisis often decides what is dangerous before it decides what is true. This committee asks delegates to reverse that process. They must slow down fear, examine evidence, protect procedure and decide whether accusations reveal real danger or manufacture it.

A claim based on what someone says they heard, rather than direct evidence.
